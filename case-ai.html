February 2026 survey of both teams.

Atomic AI adoption, in four phases

One rule: give designers the leverage engineering had, then let them build tools for their own pain.

  1. March Access VS Code, Git and Claude Code for every designer. Weekly sessions on GitHub, branches and pull requests. Four designers committing within four weeks.
  2. April to June Atoms One pain point, one slash command: find-figma, figma-annotate, design-qa, and bind-tokens. Nearly 10,000 token bindings, zero errors, in a designer's first pull request.
  3. July to August Pipeline Commands chained into an 8-stage pipeline, intake to handoff, taught through an Education Hub. Then two Figma plugins and an intake bot. More than 30 tools, in three programming languages.
  4. September to October Production Ship to engineering from the prototype; Figma polish in parallel. A bridge imports prototypes into Figma as real library instances. Three core screens shipped from prototypes.

The foundation under the pipeline

AI output is only as good as the system it's bound to. I set three owners, took over a stalled proof of concept, and agreed the token pipeline with engineering.

Key decisions and what each one cost

Skills first, the agent last

I built a UX orchestrator agent in March, without asking designers what they needed. I shelved it and inverted the order.

Traded off: our most demo-able thing, for small tools designers asked for.

Every tool earns its place

Every tool ends in adopt, adopt-with-limits or drop, backed by a written pilot evaluation, scorecards and synthetic-persona runs.

Traded off: speed. The Claude Design pilot took hundreds of prompts for a single ticket, and fewer than one in ten shaped the design. It became the exploration tool, not the handoff.

Prototype-first delivery

From September, prototypes go to engineering; Figma polish runs in parallel.

Traded off: Figma is no longer the source at handoff.

Turning a critical review into owned fixes

In October, product leadership reviewed what was still broken in design delivery. Each issue got a mechanism, an owner and a status.

  • Fixed

    Prototypes looked like the product but weren't made of it

    bind-tokens, an adherence plugin that scores and rebinds, and the Figma bridge. Off the friction list.

  • Mechanism live

    Design brought in late; UI shipped without design

    In July, about one story in seven had a design link. Now "Design required" creates a design ticket, and Done fires a Design QA ticket.

  • Baseline set

    No baselines to measure against

    Time and iteration tracking since September, a weekly KPI snapshot, and a Jira ticket study.

  • Designed, not shipped

    Usability issues found by research

    Findings from moderated and synthetic-persona testing went into two redesigns.

  • Next

    Design tokens reach the frontend by hand

    Token architecture proven on a library copy; Figma-to-pull-request pipeline proven. Live migration is next.

  • Open

    Communication with engineering after handoff

    Owners named, not solved yet. Next step proposed: a developer paired with each designer.

What research found

The team's first moderated study and a synthetic-persona pilot both surfaced usability problems in core flows. Both fed two redesigns.

To a peer

Tooling does nothing on its own; adoption is the work

Adoption was uneven: one designer wrote most of the commands, and another opened most of the pull requests. Weekly sessions made the first commit possible, a few hands made the volume, and now every designer ships from prototypes. The lesson: ask designers what hurts before building for them.

Not proven yet

  • Hours saved: not measured yet; scorecards have only just started.
  • Tickets are counts, not effort: only half to two-thirds are sized at pickup.
  • Credit limits and prototype hallucinations still interrupt work.
